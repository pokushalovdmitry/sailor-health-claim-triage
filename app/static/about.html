This tool is a prototype. Every number behind it comes from a synthetic, illustrative dataset -- not Sailor Health's real claims history. See the last question below for more on that.

Most denied claims that never get appealed weren't hard cases. They were just never looked at closely enough to decide if pursuing them was worth the time. Industry data backs this up: appealed denials win far more often than the low overall appeal rate would suggest, which means the real problem isn't winning, it's deciding.

This tool reads a denial notice and tells you, in plain language with real numbers behind it, whether an appeal is worth pursuing and what to expect if you do.

Just the denial notice itself, as a PDF. Upload it and the tool takes it from there.

Denial notices look different from every payer. Some are dense letters, some are structured tables, and the wording varies even when the reason is the same. Only the denial codes themselves are standardized, not the layout.

So the tool reads for meaning instead of expecting a fixed template. It pulls the denial reason, payer, plan, dates, and billed amount regardless of how the notice is laid out.

Three steps. First, the tool extracts the key details from the notice: denial reason, payer, plan, dates, and billed amount. Second, it searches the comparison dataset for past denials that match those details closely and pulls out the subset that looks most like this one. Third, it uses that subset to estimate the odds of winning, the likely payout, and the cost and time involved, and turns that into a recommendation.

A set of past denials and how they turned out. Each record includes the denial reason, the payer and plan, whether an appeal was filed and won, how much came back when it won, how long it took, and what it cost to pursue.

This is the dataset the second step above searches. A new claim gets matched against past denials with the same denial type, payer, plan, and whether the service happened this year or last year, and the estimate comes from that matched group.

Right now, this dataset is synthetic. It was built to behave the way real claims data behaves (technical errors resolve faster and win more often than clinical disputes, for example) but it isn't Sailor Health's actual claims history. More on this in the last question below.

The tool multiplies the billed amount by the win probability and the payout percentage to get an expected dollar recovery, subtracts the typical cost of pursuing an appeal like this one, and adjusts for how long that money is expected to take to arrive. What's left is the net value of pursuing the appeal, which drives the recommendation.

NPV stands for net present value. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in five months, since money now can be used now. The tool discounts the expected payout by how long the appeal is likely to take, so a claim that pays well but takes a long time doesn't look the same as one that pays the same and resolves fast.

The discount rate is fixed at 10% annualized for this prototype.

Because pursuing an appeal has a real cost, and not every appeal is worth that cost. A low dollar claim, or one from a category with a low historical win rate, can end up with a net value close to zero or negative once the cost of pursuing it is subtracted. That's the tool working as intended, not a failure. Its entire purpose is making that call quickly and consistently instead of leaving it to whoever happens to be looking at the claim that day.

Some claims have strong economics but a short window to file, based on the payer's appeal deadline. Rather than quietly turning that into a "don't pursue," the tool flags it separately: pursue, but file quickly. The deadline itself comes from a separate reference table of payer rules, not from the notice, since appeal deadlines are usually a matter of payer policy rather than something printed on the document.

The tool checks first. If the document is missing a denial reason, missing payer information, or clearly isn't a denial notice at all, it says so directly instead of guessing and giving you a result that doesn't mean anything.

No. Every win rate, payout percentage, appeal cost, and filing deadline in this prototype is synthetic, built to be internally consistent rather than measured from real claims. The next step is integrating Sailor Health's actual denial data in place of this synthetic dataset, and expanding the number of parameters the tool searches on to get a more specific match for each claim. The way the tool reads notices and runs the math would stay the same. Only what it's comparing against, and how finely it can match, would improve.
